### 1. Definition
A Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attack is a cyberattack that overwhelms a server, network, website, or online service with excessive traffic from multiple sources, disrupting availability for legitimate users.

- DoS (Denial of Service): An attack originating from a single source or a limited number of sources.
    
- DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service): An attack originating from many distributed sources simultaneously.
### 2. How a DDoS Attack Works
A DDoS attack typically involves three main components:
1. Attacker: Coordinates and directs the attack against a target.
2. Botnet: A network of compromised devices controlled by the attacker. Devices may include computers, servers, routers, and IoT devices.
3. Victim: The targeted server, network, or online service.

Attack process:
1. The attacker gains control of multiple devices, forming or using a botnet.
2. The attacker instructs the compromised devices to send traffic toward the target.
3. The incoming traffic overwhelms available resources, such as bandwidth, processing capacity, or connection limits.
4. The service slows down, becomes unavailable, or crashes, preventing legitimate users from accessing it.
### 3. Common DDoS Characteristics
- Distributed Traffic: Requests originate from multiple devices and locations.
- Resource Exhaustion: Consumes bandwidth, CPU, memory, or connection capacity.
- Service Disruption: Causes slow performance, outages, or complete unavailability.
- Compromised Devices: Botnets often contain malware-infected computers, routers, and IoT devices.
### 4. Real-World Example: Dyn Attack (2016)
In October 2016, the DNS provider Dyn suffered a major DDoS attack involving the Mirai botnet.

- Botnet: Mirai, which compromised numerous IoT devices, including cameras and routers.
- Target: Dyn's internet infrastructure, including DNS services.
- Impact: Many major websites and online services experienced accessibility problems across parts of the United States and Europe.

Key lesson: Insecure IoT devices can be compromised and used collectively to disrupt critical internet infrastructure.
### 5. Impact of DDoS Attacks
- Financial Losses: Lost revenue from service downtime, especially for e-commerce and online services.
- Reputational Damage: Reduced customer trust and potential loss of customers.
- Operational Disruption: Interrupts business operations and essential online services.
- Service Unavailability: Prevents legitimate users from accessing websites and applications.
- Secondary Attacks: May distract security teams while attackers attempt other malicious activities. However, a DDoS attack does not automatically mean another attack is occurring.
### 6. DDoS Mitigation
Common defensive measures include:
- Traffic Filtering: Identify and block malicious or unwanted traffic.
- Rate Limiting: Restrict the number of requests accepted within a given period.
- DDoS Protection Services: Use specialized providers to detect and absorb or filter attack traffic.
- Content Delivery Networks (CDNs): Distribute traffic across infrastructure to improve resilience.
- Load Balancing: Distribute incoming requests across multiple servers.
- Monitoring and Alerting: Detect abnormal traffic patterns and respond quickly.
- Redundancy and Capacity Planning: Reduce the likelihood that a single overloaded component will cause a complete outage.
### Key Takeaways
- A DDoS attack targets availability, one of the three pillars of the CIA triad.
- A botnet is a collection of compromised devices used to generate distributed attack traffic.
- DDoS attacks exhaust network or system resources, preventing legitimate access.
- IoT devices can be recruited into botnets when they lack adequate security.
- DDoS attacks can cause financial, operational, and reputational damage.
- Mitigation focuses on filtering traffic, distributing workloads, and maintaining service availability.

Remember: DoS overwhelms a target with traffic from a source; DDoS overwhelms it with traffic from many distributed sources. The primary objective is to disrupt availability, not necessarily to steal data.
