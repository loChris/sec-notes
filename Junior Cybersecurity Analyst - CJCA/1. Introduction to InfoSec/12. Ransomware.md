### 1. Definition
Ransomware is a type of malware that encrypts files or systems, making them inaccessible until the victim is given a way to recover access. Attackers demand a ransom, often in cryptocurrency, in exchange for a decryption key or other promised assistance.

- Primary motive: Financial gain through extortion.
- Common targets: Individuals, businesses, hospitals, government agencies, and critical infrastructure.
- Main objective: Deny access to data or systems to pressure victims into paying.

Note: Some ransomware attacks also steal data and threaten to publish it, even if the victim can restore their files.
### 2. How a Ransomware Attack Works
1. Initial Access: Attackers gain entry through methods such as phishing emails, malicious attachments, exploited vulnerabilities, or compromised credentials.
2. Execution: The ransomware runs on the victim's system.
3. Encryption: Files, databases, or other accessible resources are encrypted, preventing normal access.
4. Ransom Demand: Attackers display instructions demanding payment, often with a deadline.
5. Recovery or Further Extortion: Victims attempt to restore systems, negotiate, or respond to the demand. Payment does not guarantee successful recovery or prevent stolen data from being exposed.
### 3. Real-World Example: WannaCry (2017)
- Attack: A global ransomware outbreak that affected more than 200,000 computers across over 150 countries.
- Vulnerability: Exploited a Windows vulnerability associated with the SMBv1 file-sharing protocol.
- Contributing factor: Many affected systems had not installed the relevant security updates.
- Impact: Disrupted organizations worldwide, including hospitals within the UK's National Health Service (NHS), where appointments and procedures were canceled.
- Ransom demand: Initially, approximately $300 in Bitcoin per affected system, with demands potentially increasing.

Key lesson: Unpatched vulnerabilities can allow malware to spread rapidly across networks, causing widespread disruption.
### 4. Types of Ransomware Extortion
- Encryption-Based Ransomware: Encrypts files to deny access.
- Double Extortion: Encrypts data and steals sensitive information, threatening to publish it.
- Data Extortion: Steals information and threatens to expose it, potentially without encrypting files.
### 5. Impact of Ransomware
- Operational Disruption: Systems and business services become unavailable.
- Financial Losses: Includes downtime, recovery costs, incident response, and potential ransom payments.
- Data Loss: Files may be permanently lost if recovery options are unavailable or compromised.
- Data Exposure: Stolen confidential information may be leaked or sold.
- Reputational Damage: Customers and partners may lose trust.
- Safety Risks: Disruptions in healthcare and other critical services can endanger people.
- Further Targeting: Paying a ransom does not guarantee recovery and may not prevent future attacks.
### 6. Ransomware Prevention and Mitigation
- Regular Patching: Install security updates promptly to address known vulnerabilities.
- Backups: Maintain regular, tested backups, including offline or immutable copies protected from unauthorized modification.
- Email Security: Train users to recognize phishing attempts and filter malicious attachments and links.
- Endpoint Protection: Use security tools to detect and block malicious activity.
- Access Control: Apply least privilege and restrict unnecessary administrative permissions.
- Network Segmentation: Limit malware's ability to spread between systems.
- Incident Response: Isolate affected systems, investigate the incident, and restore services using trusted recovery procedures.
### Key Takeaways
- Ransomware is malware that denies access to data or systems, usually to extort money.
- Phishing and unpatched vulnerabilities are common attack vectors.
- Ransomware can spread across networks and disrupt entire organizations.
- Attackers may steal data in addition to encrypting it.
- Paying a ransom does not guarantee data recovery or confidentiality.
- Backups, patching, access controls, and incident response are essential defenses.

Remember: Ransomware primarily threatens the availability of information by denying access to files and systems. When attackers also steal data, it threatens confidentiality as well.
